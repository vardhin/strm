7.1 Validation and Output Review

The noise column experiment proceeded through three parts, each building on the
discoveries of the previous one. The structure was intentional: Part 1 established
primitive vocabulary under junk columns, Part 2 extended that to physics relationships
under correlated noise, and Part 3 synthesized those discoveries into a multi-step
composition. The progressive dependency between parts means that a failure in any
earlier part would cascade into the later ones, making the experiment a rigorous end-
to-end test of the entire pipeline rather than a collection of independent checks.

Part 1 established SQUARE and FORCE in the registry. SQUARE was presented with
three input columns where two were purely random noise. The search correctly identified
that only one column contributed to the output and recovered MUL(x, x) through single-
column routing into both argument slots. FORCE was presented with four columns in
interleaved order — junk, mass, junk, acceleration — and the search recovered
MUL(mass, acceleration) by eliminating both noise columns through NULL routing.
Both functions were registered at layer 1 with the correct arity and no fitted constants.

Part 2 established KE and PE. KE was presented alongside a column containing
mass plus a random perturbation in [-3, 3], creating a multicollinearity condition.
Several candidate compositions passed the training split but failed the holdout split
because they incorporated the correlated column, whose imperfect correlation was
insufficient to generalize. The search eventually found the correct form — MUL applied
to mass and SQUARE applied to velocity — and the constant fitting stage recovered
k=0.5 with R2 above 0.999. PE was recovered similarly with k=9.81, the gravitational
constant, fitting to within five decimal places of the true value.

Part 3 presented TOTAL_E with junk columns interspersed among mass, velocity, and
height. With KE and PE already registered, the search found the correct composition
at depth 2: ADD(KE(mass, velocity), PE(mass, height)) with appropriate column
routing to direct each subfunction to its relevant inputs. The registered function achieved
R2 above 0.999 against the full example set including holdout.

7.1.1 Representative Output Interpretation

A successfully registered function is stored in the database as a JSON composition
graph and rendered in the frontend as a human-readable symbolic expression. KE, for
example, is stored as a two-step composition: first SQUARE applied to the velocity
column, then MUL applied to mass and the result of the squaring step. A multiplicative
constant of 0.5 is stored alongside the composition and applied after all steps evaluate.
The frontend renders this as MUL(SQUARE(v), m) * 0.5, which a physicist or engineer
can verify against the known formula immediately without any knowledge of how the
system works internally.

TOTAL_E is stored as a two-step parallel composition: KE applied to the mass and
velocity routing, PE applied to the mass and height routing, ADD combining both
results. The human-readable form makes explicit which input columns feed into which
registered subfunction and confirms that none of the noise columns appear anywhere
in the composition graph. The absence of noise columns is not something the system
was told to achieve — it emerged from the validation criterion rejecting candidates that
incorporated them.

The R2 score displayed alongside each registered function provides a quantitative
summary of how well the discovered expression accounts for the variance in the training
examples. All functions registered during the noise experiment achieved R2 above 0.999
against the full example set, confirming that the discovered expressions generalize and
are not artifacts of the training split selection.

7.1.2 Graphs and Inferences

The streaming training log produced during each search session provides a step-by-step
record of the TRM's reasoning. In the early steps of a search, the TRM's top-5
predictions tend to be distributed broadly across the vocabulary, reflecting the high
temperature at the start of search and the zero-initialized carry state. As the carry warms
across steps, the distribution sharpens and the correct primary and secondary functions
move toward the top of the ranked list.

For the KE task, the TRM consistently proposed MUL as the primary function and
SQUARE as the secondary function by step 3 or 4 of search, before the correct
composition was validated. The composition type head showed increasing probability
on the nested type — consistent with the structure ADD(SQUARE(v), ...) — across the
same steps. This convergence pattern confirms that the carry state is integrating useful
information from the encoded examples across steps rather than producing independent
predictions at each step.

Near-miss logging during the multicollinearity condition revealed candidates that used
the correlated noise column. These candidates achieved R2 values in the range of 0.97
to 0.99 against the training split, which is high enough that a purely confidence-based
acceptance criterion would have accepted them. The executor-based holdout validation
rejected all of them, and the logged R2 values against the holdout split for these
candidates were consistently lower — in the range of 0.90 to 0.96 — confirming that
the generalization gap was detectable and that the holdout split was doing the work
it was designed to do.

For tasks where the TRM had been trained through many replay passes on similar
functions, the halt head triggered early exit at supervision step 4 or 5 rather than
completing all 16 steps. The total training time for these tasks was proportionally
reduced. This behavior is a form of learned curriculum efficiency that emerges from
the training dynamics: the model becomes faster at familiar problem types as it
accumulates experience through replay.

7.2 Discussion

The most significant finding from the noise experiment is the TOTAL_E synthesis result.
Discovering TOTAL_E from raw examples without KE and PE in the registry would
require finding, in a single search pass, a five-step composition from primitives
involving three input columns, a multiplicative constant, a squaring operation, an
intermediate multiplication, and a final summation. At the default search depth and step
budget, this is not tractable. With KE and PE already registered, the same relationship
is a two-step depth-2 composition. The registry reduced what would have been an
unreachable target into a straightforward search.

This confirms the central architectural claim: accumulated discoveries make harder
problems qualitatively reachable, not merely faster to solve. A system with a fixed
primitive vocabulary and no growing registry cannot exhibit this behavior regardless
of how its search budget is scaled, because the required subexpressions simply do not
exist as named, callable entities in its search space.

The multicollinearity result confirms the value of the holdout split as a verification
component rather than a convenience. Removing the holdout and accepting candidates
on training-split accuracy alone would have produced incorrect registrations in Part 2
of the experiment. The registered functions would have incorporated the correlated
noise column, their R2 on fresh data would have been lower than the acceptance
threshold, and Part 3 would have built TOTAL_E on top of these incorrect foundations
— producing a cascading error that the experiment structure was specifically designed
to expose.

One aspect of the deep supervision training that was not anticipated at design time is
how quickly the carry state converges for tasks the TRM has encountered many times
through replay. For primitive tasks presented after ten or more replay passes, the halt
head triggered early exit consistently by step 4 or 5. The total supervision steps
consumed per replay epoch for these tasks dropped to roughly one-third of the default
16. This means the cost of replay training does not scale linearly with the number of
replay passes as the registry grows — frequently replayed tasks become cheaper per
epoch, partially offsetting the cost of maintaining a larger replay buffer.

The head initialization strategy near the existing mean produced a measurable
improvement in how quickly newly registered functions appeared in TRM predictions
after expansion. With random initialization, newly registered functions rarely appeared
in top-5 predictions until after two or three full replay passes. With mean-centered
initialization, they appeared in top-5 predictions in the first replay pass in all tested
cases. This matters for the synthesis task: if KE and PE were not reliably proposed by
the TRM immediately after registration, the TOTAL_E search would not benefit from
them appearing in the depth-3 candidate set.

7.3 Limitations and Risk Observations

The system is sensitive to the quality and coverage of the provided examples. Too few
examples increase the risk that a spurious relationship passes the holdout split simply
because the holdout is too small to expose the generalization gap. The minimum
practical example count is around ten, with at least four in the holdout. Examples with
narrow value ranges may prevent discovery of relationships that only manifest clearly
at the boundaries of the input domain.

The search depth is bounded by max_depth=3 and the step budget by max_steps=10
by default. Relationships that require four or more composition steps from primitives,
or that require composing three or more previously registered functions in novel
arrangements, may not be discoverable within these limits. Increasing max_steps
extends discovery time roughly linearly; increasing max_depth expands the candidate
set at depth 3 and can increase per-step search time if the vocabulary is large.

The constant fitting mechanism supports only a single multiplicative or additive constant
per function. Relationships requiring multiple independent constants — such as a linear
model y = a*x + b where both a and b must be fitted — cannot be expressed in the
current framework. The additive constant fit requires the offset to be truly constant
across all examples, which means even slightly non-constant offsets will be rejected.
This is a deliberate conservatism rather than an oversight: accepting noisy offsets would
reduce confidence in the registry's correctness guarantees.

The primitive vocabulary does not include transcendental functions. Relationships
involving sin, cos, exp, or log are not discoverable from the current primitive set.
Extending the vocabulary to include these would expand the scope of discoverable
relationships but would also expand the search space, potentially requiring a larger step
budget for problems that do not involve transcendental functions.

7.4 Practical Implications

For laboratory instrumentation, the system provides a path from raw sensor measurements
to a verified symbolic calibration equation. The equation is expressed in terms of the
input variables and is auditable by the calibration engineer without any machine learning
expertise. The discovered expression can be copied directly into firmware, embedded
in a calibration certificate, or incorporated into a data processing script, all without
any dependency on the system that produced it.

For embedded monitoring systems, the growing registry provides a mechanism for
incremental knowledge accumulation that does not require sending data to a cloud
service. As the device encounters new phenomena over its operational lifetime, it can
discover and store new symbolic relationships locally. Earlier discoveries remain
accessible as building blocks, so the system's ability to handle increasingly complex
phenomena grows with its operational history.

For research applications, the system provides a lightweight interpretable alternative to
large neural regression models for hypothesis generation from small datasets. A
researcher who suspects a power-law or product relationship between measured quantities
can submit a dataset of examples and receive a verified symbolic form within seconds.
The expression can then be tested against additional data, incorporated into a theoretical
model, or used as the basis for a follow-up experiment — all without treating the
discovery process itself as a black box.

7.5 Cost Analysis

A local deployment on a single-board computer capable of running Python 3.12 costs
in the range of 50 to 100 US dollars in hardware. No cloud subscription, API key, or
ongoing data transfer cost is involved. The software stack is entirely open-source.
Maintenance consists of periodic checkpoint and database backups, which can be
automated with a standard scheduled task.

For environments where data egress incurs compliance costs, local processing eliminates
those costs entirely. For environments where connectivity is intermittent, local processing
ensures that discovery sessions complete without network access. The SQLite database
can be copied to a replacement device in seconds, carrying the full accumulated registry
with no loss of symbolic knowledge.

The economic case strengthens as the registry grows. A registry built over a year of
calibration work contains symbolic building blocks that shorten future calibration
sessions. Each additional discovery reduces the effective cost of the next one, because
the growing vocabulary makes harder targets reachable within the same step budget
that could only reach simpler targets when the registry was smaller. This compounding
return on accumulated knowledge is the economic argument for the registry-based
architecture rather than a stateless symbolic solver.
