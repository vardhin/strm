                   8. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS

8.1 Conclusion

NSSR demonstrated that neural-guided symbolic regression can be made practical on
CPU-only edge hardware without sacrificing the interpretability that motivates the
approach. The key architectural choices — a recursive rather than deep transformer
structure, exact execution-based validation, a growing SQLite-backed vocabulary
registry, and full replay-based continual learning — each addressed a specific gap in
the prior art and worked together coherently in the final system.

The Tiny Recursive Model, adapted from Jolicoeur-Martineau's (2025) "Less is More"
framework, proved to be the right neural backbone for this setting. Achieving 42
effective transformer layers from two actual parameter layers kept the model within the
memory budget of a single-board computer while providing enough reasoning depth to
guide search toward correct symbolic compositions. The separation of the latent reasoning
state z from the answer state y, and the y-only final update step that deliberately excludes
the input, produced a model that generates progressively improving predictions across
successive forward passes — behavior that is essential for iterative search guidance and
that a standard single-pass transformer does not naturally exhibit.

The deep supervision training regime reinforced this behavior during training. By
accumulating loss across all 16 supervision steps per epoch rather than only at the final
step, the training objective directly penalized carries that produce poor predictions
mid-sequence. The result was a model whose predictions improve monotonically across
steps on familiar tasks, and whose halt head correctly signals convergence for well-
practiced tasks after as few as four or five steps, reducing the cost of replay training
for those tasks over time.

The noise column experiment confirmed the three most important design claims. The
NULL-based column elimination correctly identified and excluded irrelevant inputs in
all tested configurations, including the multicollinearity condition where a correlated
noise column provided a partial signal strong enough to fool a training-split-only
acceptance criterion. The holdout split caught every spurious candidate in Part 2 of the
experiment, confirming that the two-split acceptance criterion provides meaningful
generalization protection beyond what training accuracy alone can offer. The constant
fitting mechanism recovered k=0.5 for kinetic energy and k=9.81 for potential energy,
both to within five decimal places of their true values, with R2 exceeding 0.999 in
every accepted case.

The synthesis result in Part 3 of the experiment — recovering TOTAL_E as a two-step
composition of the previously discovered KE and PE functions — provided the clearest
demonstration of what the growing registry contributes. Without KE and PE in the
registry, TOTAL_E is a five-step composition from primitives that exceeds the default
search depth. With them, it is a depth-2 composition that the search finds in a handful
of steps. The registry did not merely speed up the discovery; it made the discovery
tractable at all within the given resource budget. This distinction is the core argument
for vocabulary growth as an architectural property rather than an optional optimization.

Full replay after each registration prevented catastrophic forgetting across all tested
retention cases. Earlier tasks remained discoverable throughout the experiment, even
after the vocabulary had grown through multiple rounds of head expansion and the model
had undergone many replay passes on newer, more complex tasks. The head initialization
strategy — new rows near the empirical mean of existing rows — ensured that newly
registered functions appeared in the TRM's top-5 predictions from the first replay pass
after expansion, which was necessary for them to contribute to depth-3 candidate
generation immediately after registration.

The registry is the most durable artifact the system produces. Model weights are a means
to an end — a tool for guiding search toward correct compositions — and they can be
retrained from scratch given the replay buffer. The registry, by contrast, accumulates
verified symbolic knowledge that is independent of the model. An equation stored in the
registry today remains correct and callable tomorrow regardless of what happens to the
model. This asymmetry between the transient nature of the neural component and the
durable nature of the symbolic registry is, in a sense, the point of the entire architecture.

8.2 Future Enhancements

Several directions for extending the system merit serious consideration.

The constant fitting mechanism currently supports only a single multiplicative or additive
scalar per function. Extending it to fit multiple constants jointly — for example,
recovering y = a*x^2 + b*x from examples where both a and b are unknown — would
expand the class of discoverable relationships substantially. This would require a more
careful acceptance criterion to prevent overfitting to small example sets, likely involving
a penalty term on the number of fitted constants relative to the number of examples.

The simplification module applies three strategies: single-function match, step pruning,
and column stripping. Adding algebraic identity simplification — recognizing that
MUL(x, CONST(1)) reduces to CONST(x), or that ADD(x, NULL(y)) reduces to x —
would produce cleaner registered expressions and prevent the accumulation of
compositionally valid but symbolically redundant functions in the registry over long
sessions.

Adaptive replay scheduling is a natural extension of the current full-replay approach.
Rather than giving every task equal weight in every replay pass, a policy that prioritizes
tasks where the TRM's current predictions are furthest from correct would direct training
effort where it is most needed. This could reduce total replay training time without
compromising retention, particularly as the registry grows to hundreds of functions and
full-buffer replay becomes the dominant cost per registration.

Cross-device registry sharing would extend the system's utility to multi-instrument
environments. A compact symbolic exchange format — essentially a serialization of the
composition graphs and fitted constants for a subset of the registry — would allow two
devices to share discovered functions without transferring any raw measurement data.
Each device could discover functions locally and share only the verified symbolic results,
enabling distributed knowledge accumulation while fully preserving data locality.

Typed function signatures, where each function declares the expected type of each input
and the type of its output, would allow the search to prune structurally invalid
compositions before executing them. For example, a function that produces a Boolean
output (0 or 1) should not feed into the primary argument of MUL in a context where
a continuous value is expected. Type checking at candidate generation time would reduce
the number of candidates that consume executor time only to fail at execution, improving
search efficiency as the vocabulary grows.

Human-in-the-loop acceptance provides a path toward higher-stakes deployments. Rather
than accepting a candidate automatically when it passes the validation criterion, the
system could present verified candidates to an operator for confirmation before
registration. The operator's confirmation adds a layer of domain-specific sanity checking
that the symbolic executor cannot provide: for example, catching a relationship that is
mathematically correct on the provided examples but physically implausible given the
known properties of the measured system. This is particularly relevant in safety-critical
instrument calibration contexts where an incorrect registry entry could propagate errors
through many downstream computations.

The primitive vocabulary is the most consequential boundary on what the system can
discover. Adding transcendental primitives — sin, cos, exp, log — would expand the
scope of discoverable relationships to include oscillatory, exponential, and logarithmic
phenomena. The main engineering challenge is that these primitives can produce complex
floating-point behavior (infinities, NaN values, extreme sensitivities near singularities)
that the executor would need to handle gracefully. The acceptance criterion would also
need adjustment, since exact-match validation at rel_tol=1e-6 may be too strict for
expressions involving transcendental functions evaluated at large or small arguments.

Finally, the search depth and step budget are currently fixed parameters. An adaptive
search policy that increases depth and step count on each retry, guided by the best near-
miss R2 observed so far, would allocate search resources more efficiently than the
current fixed retry with temperature boost. A search that has seen near-misses with
R2 of 0.99 is close to a solution and deserves more steps; a search that has seen near-
misses with R2 of 0.5 is far from a solution and might benefit more from a different
starting carry than from additional steps at the same depth.
